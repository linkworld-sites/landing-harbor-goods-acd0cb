title: Why We Number Every Bag

---

Most leather goods are sold the way clothing is sold: a name, a season, a
story about a trend. We build them the way a machinist builds a part — cut to
a fixed pattern, measured against a tolerance, and given a number instead of
a name that will feel dated in five years.

That number is JM-001, JM-002, and so on. It isn't a serial code for
inventory — it's a record. We track the hide it came from, the hand that cut
it, and the date it left the bench. If a seam ever fails, we don't ask what
"collection" it belonged to. We look up the number and open the file.

## A material study, not a mood board

Full-grain leather doesn't perform on a schedule. It creases where you carry
it, darkens where your hand rests, and holds a scent long after the box it
shipped in is gone. None of that shows up in a glossy studio shot. It shows
up over months of use — which is why we started keeping our own long-term
photographs of the pieces we build, the same way a tool company documents
wear on a wrench.

You'll see that record on our site as **the Patina Ledger** — four stages of
one bag, tracked from month one to year eight. It's not styling. It's the
plainest kind of proof we know how to offer: here is what the material
actually does, over the years you'll actually own it.

## Built once means built to be checked

We don't have a returns problem to hide, so we don't write around one.
Every dimension on the site is a real dimension. Every stitch count is a
real stitch count. If a spec sheet feels like overkill for a leather bag,
that's exactly the point — it's the same respect we'd want from anyone
building something meant to outlast us.
