Most people shopping for a leather bag are really shopping for two things at
once: how it looks today, and whether it'll still be worth carrying in ten
years. Those two questions get answered by completely different details —
and the second one is the part that's easy to miss in a product photo. Here's
what we'd actually check, piece by piece, before buying a bag meant to
outlast a decade of use.

## Check the leather grade first

"Leather" on a tag can mean almost anything. **Full-grain leather** is the
outer layer of the hide, left intact rather than sanded and coated — it's
the only grade that develops a real patina instead of just wearing thin.
Ask what grade a bag is cut from before anything else; everything downstream
of that answer — how it ages, how it repairs, how long it lasts — follows
from it.

  as continuous across the whole bag.
- **[The Companion Wallet](/shop)** (€120) is built from full-grain
  offcuts, hand-burnished rather than machine-finished — proof that "full-grain"

## Look at how the seams are actually made

Glued or single-thread seams fail all at once, usually at a corner under
load. A **saddle-stitched** seam uses two needles and one thread locked
through each hole, so if one stitch is ever cut, the row next to it holds.
The Weekender's gusset is saddle-stitched for exactly this reason — a duffel
gets loaded and dropped in a way a wallet never does, and the seam has to
survive that for decades, not seasons.

## Check the tanning, not just the color

How a hide is tanned determines how it ages, and it's rarely on the label.
**The Ledger Briefcase** (€580) is oak-bark tanned — a slower vegetable
tanning process that produces a firmer, denser hide than the chrome tanning
used in most mass-market bags. That density is what lets it hold a
structured shape over a laptop and years of commuting instead of slumping.

## Check the hardware

Hardware is the first thing to fail on a cheap bag, because it's usually
plated zinc — it pits and discolors the moment the coating wears through.
**Solid brass** tarnishes instead, and tarnish polishes off. The Weekender's
turn-lock and the Ledger Briefcase's toggle closure are both solid brass,
which means the closure you use daily is built on the same decades-long
clock as the leather around it.

## Ask what "durable" is actually being measured against

A bag that lasts for decades isn't competing with this season's accessory
drop — it's competing with the bag it replaces, which might be five bags
from now if you buy for a trend cycle instead of a hide. That's the real
comparison between durable leather goods and fast-fashion accessories: not
which looks better on day one, but which one you're still carrying, unrepaired
and un-replaced, in year eight.

We track that literally rather than asserting it — the Patina Ledger on

two, and year eight, so the aging claim is something you can look at instead
of take our word for.

## A quick checklist before you buy

1. **Grade** — is it full-grain, and is that stated plainly, not just
   implied by the word "leather"?
2. **Stitching** — saddle-stitched seams at stress points, or glue and a
   single machine line?
3. **Tanning** — vegetable or oak-bark tanned for density, or fast chrome
   tanning for a lower price?
4. **Hardware** — solid brass that can be repolished, or plated metal that
   pits once the coating wears?
5. **Proof** — can the maker show you the bag aging, or only tell you it will?
